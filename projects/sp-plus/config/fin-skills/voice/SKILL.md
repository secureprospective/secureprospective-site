# Learning how the advisor writes

Anything Fin writes for an advisor goes out under their name. If it does not
sound like them, they have to rewrite it, and an assistant whose drafts always
need rewriting is worse than no assistant. This skill is the conversation that
fixes that, once, so that everything afterwards starts in the right voice.

The result is one page, **Documents/Fin/Notebook/voice.md**, with `kind:
profile`. The email skill and the marketing skill both read it before they write
a word.

## Run it as a conversation

Ask **one question at a time** and react to the answer before moving on. A list
of eight questions in a single message reads as a form, and people fill in forms
badly. The whole thing should feel like five minutes with a colleague, not an
onboarding wizard.

Say at the start what this is for and how long it takes: you are learning how
they write so that drafts come back sounding like them, and it is a few minutes.

## What to find out

Cover these. Skip anything they have already told you, and stop early if they
are getting impatient; a half-finished profile is far better than an abandoned
one.

1. **Who they mostly write to.** Clients near retirement, business owners,
   people referred by an existing client, other advisors. Who is on the other
   end changes everything else.
2. **How formal.** Ask for a real example rather than an adjective: how do they
   open an email to a client they have known for years, and how do they open one
   to somebody they met once?
3. **How they sign off.** The exact words. People are particular about this and
   it is the fastest tell that a draft was not written by them.
4. **Long or short.** Do they write three sentences, or do they explain the
   background first?
5. **Words they never use.** Most advisors have a list: jargon they hate,
   phrases that sound like a sales script, words their compliance officer has
   told them to drop. Ask for it directly.
6. **How much explaining.** When something is technical, do they simplify it, or
   do their clients expect the detail?
7. **Where they are.** Region and whether they write the way people speak there.
8. **Anything of theirs to learn from.** If they have a recent email or letter
   they were happy with, offer to read it and pick up the pattern. This is worth
   more than any of the answers above.

## The assessments question

Ask whether they have ever done a business or working-style assessment, and name
a few so the question is answerable:

> Have you ever done one of those working-style assessments, like Kolbe A, DISC,
> CliftonStrengths, Myers-Briggs, Working Genius or Predictive Index? If you have
> the result handy it tells me a lot about how you like to communicate.

If they have one, ask what it said in their own words, and use it the way they
describe it. A Kolbe A result that is high in Quick Start usually means they want
the point first and the background second. A high Fact Finder usually means they
want the detail included rather than summarised away. Take that as a hint about
pacing and structure, and check it against the examples they gave you.

Two rules about this, and they are not negotiable.

- **You are not assessing them.** You are recording what they tell you about
  themselves. Never infer a personality type from how they talk to you, never
  offer an interpretation of their result, and never suggest they take one.
- **It shapes tone only.** An assessment result says nothing about what advice is
  suitable for anybody, and it never goes near a recommendation.

If they have not done one, that is a perfectly good answer. Move on without
comment.

## Writing the profile

Write `Documents/Fin/Notebook/voice.md` with `kind: profile`. Write it as
instructions to yourself, in plain sentences, so that reading it later is enough
to write in their voice:

```
## How to write for this advisor

Open warmly, one line, no "I hope this email finds you well".
Point first, then the background.
Short paragraphs, three or four sentences at most.
Never say "solution", "product suite" or "reach out".
Sign off exactly: "Talk soon, --"
Clients are mostly within ten years of retiring; explain terms the first time.
```

Keep it to one page. A profile nobody can hold in their head does not get
followed.

**Their own name is allowed** here, because it is their notebook and their own
signature. Beyond that the notebook rule applies as it does everywhere else:
no names, no email addresses, no phone numbers, no client detail of any kind.
If they hand you one of their own emails to learn from, take the pattern and
leave every name behind.

## Afterwards

Tell them it is saved, and that they can change it any time by saying so. Then
offer to put it to work straight away: "Want me to draft something with it, so
you can see whether I've got you right?" Showing beats describing.

When a later draft comes back wrong, treat that as new information. Ask what was
off, update the profile, and say you have updated it.
